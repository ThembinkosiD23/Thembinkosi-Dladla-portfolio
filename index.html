The 4IR & 5IR

My understanding of the Fourth and Fifth Industrial Revolutions — how they differ, how we got from one to the other, and what it means for society.

What is the Fourth Industrial Revolution?

Before I started researching this, I thought the 4IR was just another way of saying "AI and robots." It's more than that. The 4IR is about how a whole group of technologies — AI, IoT, big data, cloud, robotics, biotechnology — are starting to work together.

What makes it different from the 3IR (computers and the internet) is that systems are becoming intelligent, connected, and increasingly autonomous. In the 3IR, a computer was a tool. In the 4IR, systems can collect data, analyse it, learn from it, predict outcomes, and sometimes take action without a human in the loop.

Simple way to remember it: 4IR = Technology + Data + Connectivity + Intelligence + Automation.

The Role of the World Economic Forum

The WEF didn't invent 4IR — the technologies developed independently. But it played a huge role in shaping how the world talks about it. Its founder, Klaus Schwab, popularised the term in his 2016 book The Fourth Industrial Revolution.

Since then, the WEF has used its annual Davos meeting and reports to frame global conversations about AI, automation, the future of work, and skills development. One of its biggest contributions has been highlighting the need for reskilling and upskilling as automation changes the kinds of jobs available.

From 4IR to 5IR

The 5IR isn't a replacement — it builds on the 4IR. The technologies stay the same, but the emphasis shifts. Where 4IR was largely technology-centred, 5IR is increasingly human-centred.

4IR asks "What can technology do?" while 5IR asks "How can technology improve human life while remaining ethical, sustainable and inclusive?"

4IR vs 5IR

4IR Focus 5IR Focus
Technology-focusedHuman-focused
AutomationHuman-machine collaboration
Efficiency and productivitySustainable productivity and wellbeing
Autonomous systemsHuman oversight and governance
Economic growthInclusive and sustainable growth
Data-drivenResponsible data use

The three big ideas of 5IR are human-centred, sustainable and resilient.

Potential Benefits

  • Healthcare: AI can help detect diseases earlier. IoT devices can monitor patients remotely — especially important in South Africa where specialist access is uneven.
  • Education: Personalised learning platforms can adapt to a student's pace.
  • Productivity: Automating repetitive tasks frees people for creative and interpersonal work.
  • Safety: Robots can be sent into dangerous environments like mines or chemical plants.
  • Accessibility: Digital services can reach people in remote areas.
  • Sustainability: AI and IoT can help monitor energy, water and pollution.

Social Challenges

  • Job displacement: Some skills become less valuable while new ones become essential — and not everyone can retrain quickly.
  • Digital divide: In South Africa, not everyone has reliable internet, devices or digital education.
  • Cybersecurity and privacy: More connected systems mean more attack surfaces.
  • Algorithmic bias: If the data is biased, the system's decisions will be too.
  • Surveillance: Centralised data creates centralised power.
  • Data sovereignty: Where data lives matters — this is why POPIA and geopatriation are relevant.

My View

The 4IR and 5IR aren't just about technology. They're about power, access and ethics. As IT professionals, we can't just build things and hope for the best — we need to ask who benefits, who gets left behind, and what could go wrong. That shift won't happen by itself. It depends on choices — by governments, by companies, by people like me who are entering the field.
