South Africa and the 4IR / 5IR

A Position on Inclusive, Human-Centred Progress

Thembinkosi Dladla

Introduction

South Africa is at a point where its future path depends heavily on choices we make right now. The Fourth Industrial Revolution is no longer something that will happen in the future. It is happening now. Technologies like artificial intelligence, big data, the Internet of Things and cloud computing are already changing how economies work and how societies function. These changes are reshaping jobs, how services are delivered and how countries compete globally. Now a new phase is emerging the Fifth Industrial Revolution, or 5IR. This next stage puts focus on human-machine collaboration, on customisation for individuals and on bringing back human values into technology systems (Sihlali, 2025).

This paper argues that South Africa in 2026 stands at a crossroads. The country has set goals and invested heavily in digital infrastructure. Progress is uneven. Deep problems remain, especially when it comes to skills development, inequality and energy security. These issues hold the nation back even as it tries to keep up with technological transformation.

South Africa cannot treat the 4IR and the 5IR as technology projects. If we do, we risk creating something like a "house of cards" impressive from the outside, but which will fall apart because it leaves too many people behind (Yeboua, 2026). Instead, these revolutions must be seen as tools for economic growth. They should help everyone participate, not a few. Without inclusion, any advancement built on technology will be unstable and unsustainable.

Background: Understanding the 4IR and 5IR in the South African Context

The 4IR is not a single technology but a convergence of systems that blur the lines between the physical, digital and biological spheres. It is powered by tools like artificial intelligence, big data analytics, cloud and edge computing, blockchain, the Internet of Things and 3D printing. The Fifth Industrial Revolution, which is still developing, moves the focus from being efficient to working together putting worker health and system sustainability first.

In South Africa, these changes meet a society that already faces three problems: high unemployment, poverty and inequality. Youth unemployment alone was 63.9 percent for people aged 15 to 24 in 2023. That number shows a crisis, and a serious test for any plan that relies on technology (Makgatho, 2025).

The South African government has not stayed silent on the matter. In 2019 it set up the Presidential Commission on the Fourth Industrial Revolution, known as PC4IR. This move showed that top leaders recognised how important this issue was. The recommendations from this commission have led to changes. One result is the creation of the Artificial Intelligence Institute of South Africa (AIISA). There are also AI hubs now at the University of Johannesburg, Tshwane University of Technology and the Central University of Technology. A new Defence Artificial Intelligence Research Unit started in 2025. These steps show the country is trying to build capability. The White Paper on Science, Technology and Innovation, along with the proposed National Data and Cloud Policy, add to a policy framework that looks supportive — at least in writing (Marwala, 2022).

South Africa's Current Position in 2026: Ambition Meets Reality

I see South Africa's position as a mix of leapfrogging and vulnerability. The country has shown that it can adopt and deploy frontier technologies in ways that bypass infrastructure constraints. For example, drones in agriculture in Mozambique raised crop yields by 41 percent and lowered water use by 9 percent. In Rwanda, the Zipline drone delivery network cut blood delivery times from four hours to fifteen minutes. These are examples of leapfrogging on the continent (Yeboua, 2026).

South Africa also has its examples of leapfrogging. The country deployed Television White Spaces technology to bring broadband to many people. It also launched Altron's operational AI factory powered by Nvidia technology (CSIR, 2025; ITWeb, 2025).

The data centre sector is now a sign of South Africa's digital path. The data centre market will grow from about $2.6 billion in 2025 to more than $5 billion by 2031 because of AI demand (Business Insider Africa, 2026). Microsoft announced a R5.4 billion investment in March 2025, adding to a R20.4 billion pledge. This new Microsoft investment is meant to grow cloud and AI infrastructure and to teach South Africans AI skills (Microsoft, 2026). Equinix has promised $438 million to grow its African footprint, and its Johannesburg data centre opened earlier than planned (Ecofin Agency, 2026). These big investments show that South Africa is seen as a regional hub for the digital economy.

However, this progress is uneven. The country's education system struggles to produce graduates with the skills required by the 4IR economy. A 2023 report by the Department of Higher Education and Training found that over 50% of graduates lack the skills demanded by the job market, and only 33% qualify in high-demand fields such as engineering (Makgatho, 2025). Digital literacy remains a gap: while approximately 74.7% of South Africans have access to the internet, only 17% possess digital skills that meet or exceed the global average (Daily Dispatch, 2025). This skills deficit is not merely a failure — it is a structural barrier to the kind of inclusive growth that 4IR and 5IR promise.

How South Africa Could Benefit from the 4IR and 5IR

The potential benefits of the 4IR and 5IR for South Africa are substantial, but they are not automatic. They rely on deliberate, coordinated action.

The significant opportunity lies in using the 4IR and 5IR to tackle South Africa's most persistent challenges. The 4IR offers ways to diversify an economy that has long depended on mining and traditional manufacturing. AI and big data analytics can boost productivity in agriculture, logistics and financial services. The rise of AI hubs at universities is already building a pipeline of research that can be turned into products (DCDT, 2025).

The 5IR, which stresses human-machine collaboration, points to a model where technology supports rather than replaces human labour. In the gold mining sector, 5IR-aligned technologies such as Human-Cyber-Physical Systems are being studied to improve safety and efficiency. The shift may displace low-skilled workers if it is not carefully managed (Sihlali, 2025).

The Challenges South Africa Must Overcome

The digital divide in South Africa is not one gap — it is a series of overlapping exclusions. Having access to the internet is one part. The bigger gap is in digital capability. In a society where only 17% of people have digital skills that can compete globally, that society cannot truly take part in the digital economy (Daily Dispatch, 2025).

This skills gap follows the same lines as the existing inequalities of race, class and geography. Dube and Mahhumane (2025) say that although the new technology of the 4IR is meant to make work better, many South Africans are still left behind by the divide and cannot get the full benefits of the 4IR.

Changes Necessary for South Africa to Capitalise on the Opportunity

South Africa needs an AI policy and a digital economy plan that is more than just a bureaucratic formality. The draft National AI Policy, which suggests building new bodies to oversee AI, has been called a "bureaucrat's dream" by TechCentral in 2026. The main focus should be on putting the plan into action and coordinating efforts — not on adding more oversight levels. South Africa must keep the policy human-centred, matching the 5IR spirit of teamwork and well-being.

The urgent change is in education. It needs a two-part plan. First, basic education should focus on STEM subjects, coding and digital literacy from an early age. Second, TVET colleges must be made stronger by partnering with industry so that the courses match what employers really need. The Netcampus model, which places learners in jobs after training, shows how this can work on a larger scale (Makgatho, 2025). Digital skills training should also reach communities using mobile devices and local learning hubs.

Conclusion

South Africa's position in 2026 is one of possibility and risk. The country has really come a way in creating rules and regulations, bringing in money and creating strong groups to handle the Fourth and Fifth Industrial Revolutions. But these successes are based on a shaky foundation of unfairness, a shortage of trained people, and not enough power.

The position advanced in this paper is that South Africa's 4IR and 5IR strategy must be reoriented around the principle of inclusive participation. Technology is not a silver bullet, but it is a powerful lever. If that lever is pulled in a direction that benefits only a connected elite, the result will be a more unequal society. If it is pulled in a direction that helps workers, gives people access to education and healthcare, and helps small businesses, it can help create a fairer and better future. The decision is about politics, not technology.
